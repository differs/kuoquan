# Chapter 6 · Stationary Bandit and Roving Bandit: The Ruler's True Arithmetic

A bandit bursts into a village — how does he rob it?

If he leaves tonight, he takes everything in the shortest time: grain, livestock, goods — all he can carry, and burns what he cannot. He may even kill those who resist, because he does not need them alive tomorrow.

But if he plans to put down roots here, to collect taxes for a lifetime, he will not do that.

He will leave enough seed grain, let the peasants live and work, build roads, prevent fire, and hold off other bandits. He may even lay down rules forbidding his men to kill at will — because **what he robs is no longer one season's harvest, but the tax base of the coming decades.**

The same bandit, two behaviors. Only one difference: **how long he plans to stay.**

## 1. The Core Claim

> **A ruler's behavior is not decided by his conscience, but by his time horizon.**
> **The long-term holder protects the tax base; the short-term plunderer kills the goose for the eggs — this is arithmetic, not character.**

Many are uncomfortable seeing rulers as bandits. But the value of the metaphor is this: it strips away words like "the mandate of heaven" and "justice" and forces us to ask a colder question — **over what timescale does this person calculate his own interest?**

The answer explains a great deal of seemingly contradictory history.

## 2. The Evidence

**Olson's stationary and roving bandits.**

The economist Mancur Olson drew a famous distinction: **the "roving bandit" and the "stationary bandit."**

The roving bandit moves from place to place, robs, and leaves. He has no future in the locality, so he has no reason to restrain himself — draining the tax base dry is the optimal strategy.

The stationary bandit is different: he monopolizes violence over a tract of land and intends to collect taxes long-term. Because **future returns belong to him**, he has an incentive to limit present plunder and even to provide public goods like security and roads — not out of mercy, but because such investment raises his future income.

Olson's core insight: **the seed of order is often buried in this "sustainable robbery."** One bandit who has settled down and calculates over the long term is already a vast improvement over a hundred who vanish without a trace.

**North and Thomas on the origin of property rights.**

The economic historians Douglass North and Robert Thomas pointed out that property rights often emerged not because rulers were enlightened, but because **protecting property rights served the ruler's own long-term interest.** A ruler who can guarantee "your land is yours" can raise land yields, make trade flourish, and thereby collect more tax. The institution of property rights is not a gift dropped from the sky; it grew out of the ruler's abacus.

**Tax farming.**

Many regimes in history contracted out the right to collect taxes to private parties — Rome's tax farmers and the Ottoman tax-farming system are examples. The tax farmer cares only about squeezing the most money out during the contract period; **he is not responsible for the future of the tax base.** The result is often layer upon layer of surcharges and fishing out the pond to the last fish. This proves from the reverse: **when the tax collector's time horizon is shorter than the tax base's regeneration cycle, order begins to consume itself.**

**The difference between the Mongols and the Ottomans.**

The early Mongol conquests followed a thorough roving-bandit line: resisting cities were destroyed on a large scale, because they were treated only as spoils, not as future tax sources. Only when stable rule was established (as with the Yuan dynasty) did the method of governance gradually shift toward cultivation. The Ottomans, by contrast, built comparatively early an administrative structure that lasted for centuries; their rule certainly had a cruel side, but they factored "long-term holding" into their decisions.

**Research suggests** that such differences correlate highly with how long the conqueror expects to rule. The longer the stay, the more the shift from destruction to construction — not because the heart softened, but because the ledger changed.

**The colonial models of Britain and Spain.**

A common explanation for the developmental difference between North and South America is this: after the Spaniards arrived, having secured gold, silver, and ready native labor, they tended to build **extractive** structures and drain resources back to the homeland; the British settled and cultivated more in North America, gradually growing institutions leaning more toward **property rights and self-governance.**

Acemoglu, Johnson, and Robinson further argued that what kind of institutions colonizers built, and where, depended on whether they intended to settle or merely to extract. **Settlers bring institutions to live with; extractors care only how much they can carry off.** This returns to the same variable: time horizon.

## 3. The Mechanism

### Mechanism 1: Time Horizon Decides "Protect or Drain"

A ruler's income ultimately comes from how much the people on this land can produce.

- If his rate of return covers only one season, he will choose to drain the tax base in one go.
- If his rate of return covers decades, he will choose to let the tax base keep growing.

> **Protecting the tax base is not goodwill but the self-interest of the long-sighted. Draining the tax base is not evil but the self-interest of the short-sighted.**

Both behaviors are entirely rational. The difference is not morality but **expected holding time.**

### Mechanism 2: Property Rights and the Rule of Law Often Originate in the Ruler's Long-Term Interest

This one is counterintuitive but crucial.

The rule of law and property rights sound like something the people won and used to restrain rulers. Historically they have often played that role. But their **first sprouting** often came from rulers themselves: a stationary bandit who wants to collect taxes long-term needs a predictable environment for production to expand, so he voluntarily offers a commitment that binds himself.

**This is where credible commitment enters.** The ruler says today "I will not confiscate at will," and tomorrow he must really not confiscate, or no one will believe him again. Institutions are the tool that fixes such promises in place, so that even when rulers change, the promise is not easily broken.

### Mechanism 3: Sustainable Exploitation Beats Destructive Plunder

This is the essence of stationary-bandit theory, and its coldest layer:

> **A stable exploiter brings more order to those under him than a chaotic plunderer — but this is only a byproduct, not his purpose.**

The stationary bandit does not become kinder; he becomes more **cost-effective.** He does not kill you because alive you can pay tax; he gives you security because if things are chaotic you cannot produce. The "benefit" you receive is a byproduct of someone else's arithmetic.

## 4. Costs and Counterexamples

This chapter is easily misread as "orderly exploitation is fine," so it must be spelled out.

**First, a stationary bandit is still a bandit.** Exploitation has not disappeared; it has only become sustainable. Land, labor, and output are still continually taken. The price of a sense of safety is permanent dispossession. Do not forget, just because a system runs for a long time, what it runs for.

**Second, a stationary bandit does not necessarily move toward the rule of law.** Between "long-term interest" and "secure property rights and the rule of law" lies a whole set of conditions: competitive pressure, reputation, the bargaining power of the ruled. Many stationary bandits ruled stably for centuries and exploited for centuries, while their institutions stayed stuck in the extractive stage. **Time horizon is a necessary condition, not a sufficient one.**

**Third, the real question is not "exploitation or not," but the length of the time horizon.** A short-sighted stationary bandit is more dangerous than a roving bandit — he has enough time to build an efficient machine of extraction. **Efficient squeezing is harder to escape than chaotic robbery.**

So what this chapter offers is not a standard for "a good ruler" but a **diagnostic tool**: to judge how an order will treat its people, first ask whether its decision-makers keep their books by the quarter or by the decade.

## 5. Echoes in the Age of AI

The platform is the most typical "stationary bandit" of our time.

What it collects is not rent but **data ground-rent**: you use its service, and it takes your behavior, your relationships, your attention, then monetizes them.

Different platforms have different time horizons, and so behave differently:

- Some platforms **protect the ecosystem**: cultivating developers, subsidizing content creators, restrained in running ads. Because they intend to work this land long-term, growing users and the ecosystem pays better than draining the present.
- Other platforms **kill the goose for the eggs**: drain-the-pond monetization, forced bundling, rules that change on a whim, strong-arm extraction from users. Because they treat this land as a one-time asset.

**The algorithm is the new violence in their hands.** It can extract with precision, and it can tend with precision. Which one gets used depends on whether the platform's decision cycle is **a quarter or a decade.**

Here is a danger peculiar to our time: **the extraction of data ground-rent is more hidden than the extraction of land.** When land is seized, you can see it; when attention and behavior are taken, you barely notice. So "the stationary bandit protects the tax base" can be done on a platform both gently and thoroughly — the better it tends, the longer it can harvest.

To judge whether a platform is a good "stationary bandit," the criterion is not whether it makes money, but whether it **is willing to sacrifice today's profit for the ecosystem ten years out.**

This also gives the ruled a realistic diagnostic line: **do not hope the stationary bandit turns good; change his timetable.** Let him expect to stay a little longer, make him responsible for his long-term reputation, make him understand that draining today means losing tomorrow — this works far better than appealing to his kindness. The role of institutions is precisely to turn "long-term interest" from an empty phrase into an account that can be calculated.

## 6. Chapter Summary

> **A ruler's conscience cannot be relied on; his timetable can.**
> **To know how an order will treat you, do not look at what it says, but at how long a timescale it keeps its books on.**

The stationary bandit is not a good man; he merely did long-term math. And history repeatedly proves: **an order that calculates short does not last long; an order that calculates long at least survives.**
