# Chapter 7 · Tightening the Circle: Why Purges Always End in Collapse

A purge usually begins with a seemingly rational judgment:

"Just remove this batch of people, and the ranks will be pure and things will be easier."

It always works in the short term. Those purged disappear, resistance is gone, orders travel faster. So the holder of power draws a conclusion: **this works, and it can be done again.**

But a purge is not a one-time surgery; it is more like a machine that, once started, cannot be shut off. Every turn it takes, it eats its own parts. By the time the machine stops, often even the main engine has been gnawed hollow.

## 1. The Core Claim

> **A purge is self-negating. It starts a death spiral that cannot be stopped.**
> **It means to strengthen order, but in fact tears out every load-bearing beam.**

The logic of a purge is "the tighter the circle, the safer." Its actual consequence is the opposite: **every time the circle tightens, order comes one step closer to collapse.** This chapter will unpack why this spiral accelerates on its own, until it drags down even the one who started it.

## 2. The Evidence

**Stalin's Great Purge.**

In the late 1930s the Soviet Union launched a large-scale purge. It aimed not only at the party and state apparatus but also at the military — large numbers of senior officers were arrested and executed, including Marshal Tukhachevsky and a group of marshals and senior generals.

The consequences soon showed. In the Soviet-Finnish War of 1939–1940 the Soviet army fought with extreme difficulty, exposing grave problems in the command layer. And **research suggests** that the large-scale purge of the Red Army's officer corps was one of the important causes of the Soviet army's repeated catastrophic defeats in the early war. **What was purged was not loyal enemies, but capable fighters.** To gain absolute control of the army, a regime first destroyed the army's professional capacity.

**The French Revolution: from the Terror to Thermidor.**

From 1793 the French Revolution entered the Terror. The Revolutionary Tribunal sent large numbers to the guillotine in the name of "treason" and "counter-revolution," including revolutionaries themselves — different factions accused and executed one another. Robespierre was at one point the Terror's chief driver, and in the end he too was sent to the guillotine in the Thermidorian coup.

**The Terror did not stop at "enemies"; it ate its own children.** Whoever executes others today will be next tomorrow. Once the logic of the purge starts, there is no longer any endpoint "clean enough not to be purged."

**Sulla's proscriptions in Rome.**

After seizing power, the Roman dictator Sulla published a list of "public enemies." Those on the list could be killed by anyone with impunity, and their property confiscated. This was not only political liquidation but became a tool for redistributing wealth — denunciation and private vendettas were both drawn in.

**The Thirty Tyrants of Athens.**

After the Peloponnesian War, Athens was defeated and Sparta installed an oligarchic regime known to history as the "Thirty Tyrants." It arrested and executed political opponents on a large scale and confiscated property; within little more than a year it pushed the city-state to the brink of civil war and was finally overthrown.

The two cases are centuries apart and span two civilizations, yet their structure is the same: **a liquidation launched for the sake of "purification" finally loses control and turns on its initiator.**

These cases also share a detail: a purge is never completed in one stroke. It always tests on a small scale first, then expands, then spins out of control. **Every expansion is packaged as "the necessary final step."** But the purger will never stop at any point, because he cannot prove "the rest are all clean" — he can only keep cutting, until no one dares stand before him anymore.

## 3. The Mechanism

### Mechanism 1: A Purge Destroys Information

An organization's decision quality depends on whether it can hear the truth.

The most direct consequence of a purge is that **no one dares tell the truth anymore.** Those who do are usually the first to be removed as dissidents. So those who remain learn one thing: **report good news, hide bad news.**

From then on the decision-maker lives in a retouched world. What he hears is always "the situation is excellent"; what he sees are always report sheets with no problems. **When reality finally crashes in, he has no warning left at all.**

> **A purge kills not only people but the truth. And an order that cannot hear the truth is flying blind.**

### Mechanism 2: A Purge Rewards Sycophancy and Punishes Independence

This is the cruelest one.

A purge creates a clear incentive structure:

- **The flatterer is safe.**
- **The person with independent judgment who dares dissent is in danger.**

So people of ability and backbone are eliminated, and those left are the best performers of loyalty. **The purge's selection criterion is not competence, but obedience.**

The result is adverse selection: the more an organization purges, the less those who remain dare take responsibility, and the better they are at guessing the boss's intent. A nerve center made up of "the best loyalty performers" can function in peacetime, but under a real test it fails collectively. **What they excel at is professing loyalty — not fighting, not governing, not solving real problems.**

### Mechanism 3: Fear Infects the Enforcers

A purge is carried out by a group of people. But the enforcers are afraid too: **the one who has me purge others today — will he purge me tomorrow?**

Once fear infects the enforcement layer, enforcers do two things: first, they escalate their loyalty displays, proving themselves with crueler means; second, they preemptively eliminate possible threats, erring on the side of excess. So the purge not only fails to stop, it **accelerates itself.**

The order of the knife: first the enemy, then the ally, then the comrade-in-arms, and finally the one holding the knife himself. Sulla, Robespierre, and the cadres of the Khmer Rouge all walked this road. **Fear has no boundary; in the end it finds every hand that has held the knife.**

### Mechanism 4: Survivorship Bias Makes People Believe "the Scale Is Controllable"

Why does the holder of power always feel he can control the purge?

Because he sees only the part that survived. **Those purged no longer have any chance to tell him "you were wrong."** Every successful purge gives him a false signal: see, I am in control.

This signal is fatal. It makes one believe the purge is a tool that can be operated precisely, stopped at will. But once the spiral of fear turns, control is no longer in the initiator's hands. **He thinks he is wielding the knife; in fact he is riding on its blade.**

## 4. Costs and Counterexamples

One uncomfortable fact must be admitted:

**In the short term, a purge really is "effective" for a dictator.**

It can, in a very short time, clear out political enemies, overawe potential challengers, and concentrate power. Many who launched purges found their power more secure after it, not less. If you look at only one or two years, a purge is a good bargain.

So this chapter cannot simply say "purges always fail." More precisely:

> **A purge consolidates personal power in the short term and hollows out state capacity in the long term.**

- Short term: power concentrates, opposition disappears, orders travel unimpeded.
- Long term: information distorts, talent is severed, the enforcement layer knows only how to perform, and the moment outside pressure comes the whole thing collapses.

Stalin won power, but the army he left behind paid a grievous price in the early war; the revolutionaries of the Terror held the regime, yet sent themselves to the guillotine.

**The cost does not disappear; it is only deferred.** It may not strike while the purger still lives, but it erupts all at once under his successor, when his country most needs capacity. This is why a purge is a "self-destruct device" — it hands the bill for destruction to the future.

This is also why the word "effective" must come with a timescale. **Looked at over one or two years, a purge is often effective; looked at over ten, it is often fatal.** A ruler who cares only about his own term will almost inevitably choose to purge, because the bill does not come due in his hands. This returns to the previous chapter's theme: **the decision-maker's time horizon determines how distant a consequence he is willing to answer for.**

## 5. Echoes in the Age of AI

AI has fitted the purge machine with both a stronger engine and greater risk.

**A stronger engine:** algorithmic surveillance can tirelessly screen every person's words, deeds, relationships, even emotions. It does not tire, does not hesitate, does not need loyal enforcers. In theory it can make purges **more efficient and more thorough** — what once took a massive workforce to review can now be done by one model.

**Greater risk:** this system makes rule **more dependent on surveillance.**

- Once surveillance fails, the ruler loses his judgment — because he long ago stopped hearing truth from people and only reads the scores the system gives.
- Once the algorithm errs, misjudgment spreads at machine speed. A single wrong label can in an instant brand a great many people "dissidents," and the system will not hesitate, and will not apologize.

**And AI amplifies the four mechanisms above.** It makes the truth even less sayable, makes sycophancy more easily rewarded by the algorithm, makes fear spread faster, and makes the decision-maker more prone to the illusion that "the system shows everything is normal."

**The more a purge depends on machines, the more total the collapse when the machine errs.** An order that relies on human surveillance collapses gradually; an order that relies on algorithmic surveillance can collapse in an instant — because its information, judgment, and enforcement all hang on the same system.

Technology has not changed the direction of the purge; it has only wound the death spiral tighter.

## 6. Chapter Summary

> **What makes a purge most like suicide is this: every cut believes it is removing a lesion, when in fact it is removing an organ.**

The radius of the circle is held open by trust. Every tightening of the purge permanently shaves off a ring of trust. By the time the circle is small enough that only the knife-holder remains, all that is left of the order is an empty shell that has not yet fallen.

**An order that dares not allow a dissenter inside the circle will in the end not even dare look into its own mirror.**
