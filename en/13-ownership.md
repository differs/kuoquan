# Chapter 13 · Belonging: The Arithmetic of Distributing the AI Dividend

The first ten chapters make one point: **a wide circle lives longer.**
Chapter 11 describes a danger: **AI can make a ruler believe he no longer needs the people.**
Chapter 12 describes a way out: **order must grow from the last mile by itself.**

But all of it hangs on an unanswered question:

> **If AI truly shifts the production of wealth from "the labor of the many" to "the compute of the few," then what do the many live on, and by what right are they counted "inside the circle"?**

This chapter is not about morality. It is about the ledger. For a wide circle without a material foundation is only a pleasant empty phrase. **A circle is not drawn by proclamation; it is drawn by division of the gains.**

## 1. The Core Claim

> **There are two kinds of division: dividing the "flow," or dividing the "stock."**
> **The true watershed of the AI age is not whether to divide, but what is divided.**
> **Dividing cash flow is expensive every year; dividing asset stock is built once and yields forever.**

Most people, at the thought of "who gets the AI dividend," reach for the same set of things: universal basic income, cash payments, subsidies. **These are all flow** — you must reach into the pocket again every year, and when you cannot, it stops.

What Norway and Alaska offer is another path: **first build an asset that belongs to everyone, then let the asset generate the money itself.** That is stock.

**The two paths differ in cost by an order of magnitude.** This chapter exists to make that difference clear, because it decides whether expanding the circle can survive fiscally or must remain a slogan.

## 2. The Evidence: Two Kinds of Division History Has Offered

**Flow redistribution: equal-field and land-limiting in the dynasties.**

Chinese history repeats one move: a new dynasty redistributes land after war, giving owner-farmers soil to till. This is essentially a **reset of the stock** — taking back land swallowed by powerful families and dividing it again among the many. It worked because it redistributed the "means of production" in one stroke; the tax base was then solid, and the dynasty revived.

But once the stock re-concentrates — land swallowed up again — the dynasty can only patch with **flow**: higher taxes, levies, relief. The marginal cost of patching with flow rises, and finally it collapses. **China's two-thousand-year dynastic cycle is essentially a loop of "periodic reset of the stock + chronic depletion of the flow."** (This echoes Chapter 10.)

The conclusion is plain: **an order that can reset the stock revives; an order that can only patch the flow bleeds to death.**

**Stock sharing: Norway and Alaska.**

Norway channels oil revenue into a sovereign wealth fund, accumulating assets for future generations; Alaska goes further, using oil revenue to set up a **permanent fund** and paying **an annual dividend to every resident, per capita.**

What they share: **neither tries to pull money from the treasury every year to hand to the people; both first turn the resource into an asset owned by all, then let the asset pay dividends.** Oil is a one-time resource, yet they turned it into a stock that keeps yielding.

> **Turning oil under the ground into shares in the pocket — this is the prototype of "dividing the stock."**

**The programmability of the digital yuan.**

A new experiment of our time is the **programmability** of digital currency: money can be assigned a purpose, a condition, an expiry. Its benefit is precision — subsidies reach the target directly, not intercepted by intermediary layers; its risk lies in the same place — **paying out and freezing use the same switch.** So it is a neutral tool: it can serve "universal access to the stock," or "control of the flow." Direction is set by institutions, not by technology.

**The data you produce for free.**

The last, most easily overlooked fact: the corpus, feedback, and behavioral data used to train large models are **overwhelmingly produced by ordinary people for nothing.** Every sentence you write online, every like you tap, every step you walk becomes part of the means of production, and you receive no shares.

**This is the largest "raw material taken for free" of this century.** Chapter 1 said "holding the circle open depends on external things"; here the reverse is exposed: **when ownership of the means of production is unilaterally stripped away, the circle is unilaterally narrowed.**

## 3. The Mechanism: An Account That Can Be Honestly Reckoned

### Mechanism 1: Why paying everyone is so expensive — an identity

First, an arithmetic identity that is almost impossible to avoid.

If everyone is paid a sum of cash **unconditionally**, then:

> **the share of GDP this costs ≈ the per-capita payout ÷ per-capita GDP.**

This is not an estimate; it is a definition. For "total payout = per-capita payout × population" and "GDP = per-capita GDP × population"; divide one by the other and population cancels.

Its meaning is hard:

- If you want each person to receive **half the average output**, the cost is about **50% of GDP**;
- if you want each to receive **an amount equal to the average output**, that is **100% of GDP** — collect the entire economic output, then pay it back out.

Reality adds further deductions: the distortion cost of taxation, administrative waste, and the lost incentive from "same pay whether you work more or less." **So pure-flow universal payout is not impossible, but it nearly requires tearing down the entire fiscal system and rebuilding it.**

This explains a recurring phenomenon: **the slogan of universal basic income is loud; implementation is hard.** It is not unwillingness to give, but that **the flow is too expensive.**

### Mechanism 2: Why dividing the stock is cheap — build once, yield forever

Dividing the stock avoids the identity above.

If a public asset yields a return R each year, you need only take part of that return as dividends; **the principal is untouched and the dividend continues.** You need not skim the taxpayer again each year, because the asset that produces your income is working for you.

> **Flow makes you persuade everyone anew each year that "the money should be divided"; stock asks you to persuade them only once that "the asset should be built."**

This is why the Norwegian and Alaskan models matter so much: **they turned a one-time resource into a permanent asset**, converting "dividing the money" — politically extremely difficult — into "building the asset" — a one-time, relatively easy task.

### Mechanism 3: The stock most worth dividing in the AI age is the property rights to compute and data

So what is the "oil" of the AI age? **Compute, models, and data.**

By the logic above, the smartest and cheapest way to divide is not "tax AI, then hand out cash," but:

1. **Build a public compute/data fund** — the state or a public body holds shares in compute infrastructure and key data assets;
2. **let everyone share in its returns by rule** — through dividends, public funds, or rights accounts tied to identity;
3. **establish rights at the source** — recognize personal data as a means of production, giving those from whom it is collected a share in the value they create.

**These three steps replace "flow subsidies" with "stock ownership."** It is not charity; it is returning "raw material taken for free" as "shares held by everyone."

## 4. Costs and Counterexamples

Four hard constraints must be honestly stated, or this chapter is fantasy.

**First, the return on the stock does not fall from the sky.** A fund must have money before it has returns, and the money to build it comes from extracting resource rents, taxes, or monopoly profits. **The resistance to that extraction is the real threshold of this plan.** Norway could do it largely because it has oil; a country without a one-time resource finds it harder to build stock.

**Second, establishing rights over data is operationally extremely difficult.** Who contributed a given piece of data? How is the value of a corpus attributed to tens of millions of anonymous sources? **Pricing and attribution are technical hard problems, and also the easiest excuse for vested interests to stall.** The difficulty is real, but difficulty is no reason not to do it — "hard" is often used as a shield for "unnecessary."

**Third, division can also become bribery rather than empowerment.** A payment can shut people up; a share of stock alone can give them a place. **Both are division, but the first is placation and the second is expanding the circle.** This is the deeper reason the chapter distinguishes flow from stock — **dividing the flow may be precisely the more refined means of narrowing the circle.**

**Fourth, no division plan can dodge the prior question: who decides how it is divided?** If the method is decided unilaterally from above, then what is given to you today can be withheld tomorrow. **What truly expands the circle is not how much is divided, but whether those inside the circle can take part in the rules of division themselves.** This returns to Chapter 12: **a stake is what produces a sense of ownership; only with participation is the stake reliable.**

## 5. Echoes in the Age of AI (this chapter is itself that echo)

Condense the whole logic of this chapter into one line:

> **The sharpest political question of the AI age is not whether machines will rule humanity, but whether the wealth machines produce will belong only to their owners.**

And history has ever offered only two directions:

- **Divide the flow** — cash, subsidies, relief. It is gentle, but expensive, revocable, and easily breeds people into "objects to be provided for." **This narrows the circle.**
- **Divide the stock** — shareholding, rights, public funds. It is difficult, but durable, growth-bearing, and turns people into "masters of the asset." **This expands the circle.**

**Which path is chosen decides whether AI builds an order in which people have a stake, or a machine that runs efficiently and shuts people out.**

## 6. Chapter Summary

> **A wide circle is not held open by goodwill; it is held open by "having a stake."**
> **And the highest form of having a stake is not receiving a sum each year, but owning the asset from which that sum comes.**

Flow is placation; stock is belonging.
**An order willing to divide only the flow and not the stock looks generous, but is narrowing the circle inch by inch.**

So when someone asks you "should the AI dividend be shared with ordinary people," you can put a harder question back to them:

> **The question is not whether to divide, but whether you dare let them be masters.**

And that line is the very last line of the book. The next section is the epilogue.
