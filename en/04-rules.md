# Chapter 4 · Rules: Externalizing Goodwill into Institutions

Credibility and reciprocity both rest on people.

Good people keep their word; good neighbors help each other. But good people die. A good man's son is not necessarily good. A good place turns sour the moment a bad official arrives. **If the goodness of an order depends entirely on good people happening to live inside it, that order is made of paper.**

So humanity did something momentous: **it took goodwill out of people's heads and wrote it into rules that can be seen, retained, and enforced.**

This is what institutions are. This chapter answers why they are the skeleton of durable order.

## 1. The Core Claim

> **An institution that does not depend on good people is a good institution.**

The crucial test of a good institution is not "how well it performs in good hands," but — **"will it still barely function in bad hands?"**

Because an order that must last decades or centuries will surely meet bad times and bad people. **An institution that works only in good hands stakes the fate of the order on luck.**

## 2. The Evidence

### The Code of Hammurabi: Carving Rules into Stone

Around the eighteenth century BCE, King Hammurabi of ancient Babylon issued a code of law, carved on a stone pillar and made public.

Its specific provisions may not look fair today — retaliation in kind, distinctions of rank, all spelled out plainly. But it did something unprecedented: **it turned "what may be done, what may not, and how offenses are punished" from the ruler's on-the-spot judgment into a public, fixed text.**

What did this mean? It meant a verdict no longer depended entirely on the king's mood that day, but on what was written on that pillar. **For the first time, power was leashed a little by a rule written in stone.** This was good for the ruler too: with fixed rules, subjects know how to live, and order can be replicated steadily.

### Roman Law: Letting Rules Grow on Their Own

One of Rome's greatest legacies to later ages was its law.

From early written law to the later accumulation of precedents and interpretations by judges and jurists across countless cases, Roman law grew into a vast system. **Its key feature was not how brilliant any single provision was, but that it was a self-expanding system of rules** — confronted with a new problem, it could grow a new answer under existing principles.

This was the first time a body of law behaved like a tree that grows, rather than a stone written dead. **If rules can evolve, institutions can live long.** Most later civil-law systems grew out of it.

### Qin Bureaucracy and Weber's Account

After Qin unified the six states, one of its fundamental acts was to cut all under heaven into commanderies and counties, sending officials to govern them, rather than enfeoffing nobles as hereditary lords.

Behind this stood a bureaucratic system: offices with clear duties, assessments, written records, and a chain of command from superior to subordinate. **State governance turned from "the private domain of nobles" into "a machine running by rules."**

Weber later summed up this form as **bureaucracy**: it has hierarchy, regulations, archives, and professional division of labor; it runs by impersonal rules, not the master's personal whims. He pointed out that **this rule-based form of rule is the foundation on which modern large-scale organizations can run efficiently and stably.**

Impersonality sounds cold, but that is precisely its strength: **it makes "who sits in that position" matter more than "who happens to be in that position."**

### The Imperial Examination: Making the Distribution of Power Rule-Governed

The imperial examination, which matured in China from the Sui and Tang dynasties, solved a problem that gives every large order a headache: **power and office — who exactly should get them?**

Before it, selection relied mostly on bloodline, family standing, and recommendation. The result was power monopolized by a few families, no path for the humble, and a center unable to draw talent from society. The examination **made the problem rule-governed** through one unified test: study, sit the exam, be admitted — the rules written in the open.

Its significance for rulers was twofold: **first, it brought the most literate and capable people under heaven into the system**, no longer letting them drift outside it as a threat; **second, it gave ordinary people a visible path upward**, so that even the ruled felt "there is a chance in this order for me."

The examination was not perfect — its content grew ever more rigid after the Ming and Qing, and in the end it was abolished in modern times. But the mechanism it revealed was right: **making the rules that distribute power public and procedural greatly reduces internal friction within an order.**

### The Evolution of Common Law and the Invention of the Corporation

English common law took another path: it did not rely on one great code, but precipitated, layer by layer across countless specific rulings, precedents for "how similar things should be judged later." **Rules are generated in practice and corrected in cases, evolving like living things.**

And the invention of the corporation was another milestone in institutional history. A group of strangers turned an organization into a **legal entity that can bear liability independently, own property independently, and persist independently.** Limited liability meant people no longer had to risk their entire fortune to invest; shares let strangers' capital pool into enormous force.

A corporation can outlive its founders and pack the cooperation of tens or hundreds of thousands of people into one set of rules. **The scale of the modern economy is, at bottom, amplified by an institution.**

## 3. The Mechanism

### Mechanism 1: Institutions Are Externalized Morality, Society's "External Memory"

Morality in the human brain has two fatal flaws: **it forgets, and it dies.**

Institutions moved morality out of the human brain and wrote it into law, charters, custom, and precedent. **It becomes society's "external memory" — even when those who first wrote it are long gone, the rules still speak for them.**

This is the same thing Chapter 1 called "holding the circle open requires external objects." Gods, law, money — these external objects all serve to make rules **outlast an individual life.** Institutions are the hardest of them: they do not rely on belief, they rely on enforcement.

> **A society's true moral waterline is not written in how kind its people are, but in how bad a person its institutions can restrain.**

### Mechanism 2: Institutions Are Credible-Commitment Devices That Let Strangers Trust Each Other

Chapter 2 discussed credibility resting on reputation and repeated interaction. But the overwhelming majority of cooperation in modern society happens between **strangers who will never meet a second time in their lives.**

Reputation is no longer enough, because you do not know the other party; repeated play fails too, because there may be only one transaction. Then institutions step in to back it: **an enforceable contract, a neutral court, a set of bankruptcy rules let two strangers dare to sign first and perform later.**

This is what North, Acemoglu, and others repeatedly stress: **institutions are credible-commitment devices.** They turn the anxiety of "will the other party cheat me" from private guesswork into a binding public guarantee. **They let strangers cooperate like acquaintances.**

### Mechanism 3: A Good Institution Must Be Self-Enforcing

However good a rule, if it can only be pushed by a morally perfect, infinitely energetic enforcer, it will collapse sooner or later.

So the third feature of a good institution is **self-enforcement** — that is: **compliance pays better than violation, and without external force watching every day, it keeps running on its own.**

How is this done?

- **Let those who comply benefit**: those who keep the rules get cooperation, reputation, and protection;
- **Let those who violate lose**: breaking one's word is punished, and the punishment is predictable and will actually be delivered;
- **Give enforcers an incentive to enforce**: do not let the enforcers themselves become the biggest arbitrageurs.

> **The strength of an institution lies not in how heavy the punishment is, but in "even with no one watching on site, people know what to do."**

A rule that must be maintained by constant violence is not an institution; it is a temporary order ready to collapse at any moment.

## 4. Costs and Counterexamples

Institutions are not omnipotent. They have three real weaknesses.

**First, institutions ossify.** Once rules are set, they have inertia — **path dependence.** A rule that was reasonable at the time can go out of date when circumstances change, but changing it is extremely hard, because everyone who profits from the old rule resists. The imperial examination's shift from a sharp instrument for selecting the worthy to a late-stage cage of eight-legged essays is this process. **Institutions have saved an order, and they can also drag it to death.**

**Second, institutions get captured.** Rules are made by people and enforced by people, so those with power find ways to bend the rules to their advantage. **Regulatory capture** is the norm: the regulator meant to restrain an industry is slowly co-opted by it and ends up speaking for it. **A good institution is not one without holes, but one with a mechanism for patching holes.**

**Third, stricter is not better.** Many assume that the denser the rules and the heavier the punishment, the more reliable the system. The opposite is true: **rules that are too strict and too dense breed widespread outward compliance and inward defiance** — everyone violates them, enforcers can only enforce selectively, and the rules become a tool for striking at dissidents rather than a public guardrail. When Han Fei spoke of "law," he meant public, unified, predictable — not endlessly escalating. **The goal of an institution is enforceability, not intimidation.**

To sum up, the real point of this section is one sentence: **institutions can externalize morality, but they cannot externalize everything.** They must be able to evolve, be supervised, and accommodate correction. **An institution that cannot correct itself will end up turning from the skeleton of order into its shackles.**

## 5. Echoes in the Age of AI

Algorithms are becoming a wholly new institutional form.

**It is faster.** Law is revised by the year; algorithms update by the day. Rules are written into models, decisions are made in milliseconds, executed thousands of times over — no judge, no trial needed.

**It is more ubiquitous.** Law reaches only so far; algorithms can embed themselves in every click, every recommendation, every credit decision, every loan — **it has already decided for you before you realize it.**

**It is more opaque.** This is the deadliest point. Law is public: you can read it, argue with it, appeal it. An algorithm is a black box — **you do not know why it gave you a low score, why it rejected your application, why it pushed that piece of content to you.** A rule that cannot be explained and cannot be appealed already has the full structure of oppression, however good its intentions.

> **Whoever writes the rules holds the power.** In the past, this meant legislators; in the age of algorithms, it means **the people who design the models, decide the training data, and set the objective functions.** They are not called legislators, but their rules are faster, finer-grained, and harder to challenge than law.

But the echo has more than one face.

**Algorithms can also make institutions easier to inspect.** If an algorithm is public, auditable, and reproducible, then for the first time rules can be genuinely verified at scale — **every execution is recorded, every deviation traceable.** An institution with a checkable ledger is far more reliable than one where a single person decides.

**Algorithms can also lower enforcement costs.** Many good rules in the past died of being "unaffordable to enforce," and automation can sharply cut the cost of credibility, contracts, and supervision. **This gives us a chance: to extend the reciprocity and restraint once confined to small communities to an unprecedented scale.**

The direction depends on one question — the same as in Chapter 1, Chapter 2, and Chapter 3: **is this new institution designed for everyone's convenience, or for a small few's control.**

## 6. Chapter Summary

> **A good institution is not one that runs with no bad people, but one that still barely runs with them.**

Hammurabi carved rules into stone; Rome let rules grow; Qin turned governance into a machine; the imperial examination wrote the path upward into procedure; England precipitated rules through precedent; the corporation packed strangers into one legal person. **They were all doing the same thing: moving goodwill off people who die and into things that do not easily die.**

And in the age of algorithms, this has been pushed to its extreme: for the first time, rules can run detached from people — automatically, swiftly, everywhere.

**So the question escalates too — not "do we want rules," but "who writes the rules, and is the rule-writer bound by the rules."**

A rule-maker who cannot be bound by rules becomes the institution's greatest breach.
