# Chapter 8 · The Radius and the Cost of the Circle: The Tension Between Great Harmony and Solidarity

A circle: the larger the radius, the larger the area. That is geometry.

But order is not geometry. Every step a circle expands, its internal **heterogeneity** grows by one part, its **common narrative** thins by one part, its **decision-making** slows by one part.

Expansion is never free. It keeps an invisible ledger.

## 1. The Core Claim

> **Expanding the circle has a limit.**
> **"Great harmony" and "solidarity" are not the same thing, but a real tension.**

To hold an order open to a larger population, you must accommodate more varied languages, faiths, interests, and memories. And **solidarity — the intensity of "we are one" — depends precisely on commonality.**

**The larger the circle, the more it must be held together by abstractions; the smaller the circle, the more it can be held together by face-to-face warmth.**

This is not a case against expansion. It is putting a price on expansion. Expansion without a price is called greed.

## 2. The Evidence

### Rome: after reaching the limit, it split in two

Rome expanded from a city-state into an empire spanning the Mediterranean.

During its rise, expansion was feasible. Rome invented a remarkably clever tool called **the ladder of citizenship** — first Latin rights, then full citizenship, with allies able to climb rung by rung. This let the conquered see "I too can become Roman," and so the empire rolled ever larger. **Chapter 9 will cover this in detail.**

But after reaching the limit, the bill came due.

The empire's territory was too vast: from the Rhine to the Nile, communication measured in months, the army trading loyalty for money. **The bandwidth of governance could not keep up with the expansion of territory.** By the third century CE, the empire fell into a long crisis, emperors replaced in quick succession, borders erupting on all sides.

Diocletian responded: splitting the empire into East and West, each with its own Augustus. This was an honest admission — **one center could no longer manage the entire circle.**

After Theodosius's death, the empire formally and permanently split into East and West. The Western Roman Empire fell in 476 CE; the Eastern Roman Empire lived on for another thousand years.

> **The same circle, forced to split in two, so that each half could survive.**

This was not a moral failure, but a mismatch between scale and institutions.

### The Soviet Union: trying to forge a "new Soviet man" in one generation

The Soviet Union was a deliberately designed multi-ethnic state. It wrote "nation" into its institutions: union republics, ethnic autonomy, even a "right of secession" preserved in the constitution.

In the 1920s, it pursued **indigenization**, encouraging each nationality to use its own language and cultivate its own cadres — a clever form of accommodation. But later it turned toward Russia-centered assimilation, trying to forge a single "new Soviet man."

The result? **No common narrative grew; instead the ethnic boundaries were hardened by the institutions.** When the center weakened, the 1991 collapse followed the borders of the ethnic republics — **the lines drawn to manage multiple nationalities became the fissures of the split.**

### The European Union: the deeper the integration, the greater the backlash

The EU is the contemporary experiment in peaceful expansion. From the Coal and Steel Community to the Maastricht Treaty, from the single market to the euro, then to expansion into Eastern Europe, it packed dozens of countries that once massacred one another into the same circle.

It succeeded for a long time. But each deeper layer of integration demanded that member states cede part of their sovereignty — **and sovereignty is the last vessel of national identity.**

So the backlash came: resentment of Brussels bureaucrats, anxiety about immigrants, anger at "we have been stripped of the right to decide for our own country." The 2016 British referendum to leave was the loudest eruption of this backlash.

**The more power is pushed up to the supranational level, the more easily it awakens people's anxiety about "who we are."** Between integration and identity, there too runs that line of tension.

### The Habsburgs and the Ottomans: two different endings

The Habsburg Empire was a multi-ethnic mosaic: Austrians, Hungarians, Czechs, Poles, Croats, Italians… In 1867 it struck the "Austro-Hungarian Compromise," recognizing Hungary's equal status. This eased part of the pressure but did not solve the whole — **because for every nationality granted a concession, the others demanded the same concession.** In the end, the empire came apart in the First World War, shattering into a pile of nation-states.

The Ottoman Empire took another route: the **millet system**. It governed not by territory but by religious community — Orthodox Christians, Armenians, Jews each had their own community, handling internal affairs themselves, owing the empire taxes and loyalty.

**The millet system was governance that "recognized difference."** It let the empire accommodate a highly heterogeneous population for a very long time. Its cost: the boundaries between communities were clear, each turned toward its own leader — **when the age of nationalism arrived, these communities could easily turn as a whole.**

## 3. The Mechanism

### Mechanism 1: Identity has a carrying capacity

**People's sense of "we" has an upper limit.**

As Chapter 1 said, the upper limit of face-to-face trust is roughly one hundred and fifty people. Beyond that number, identity must rely on abstract symbols — god, law, flag, leader, language.

The problem is this: **the power of abstract identity decays as heterogeneity inside the circle rises.** A group speaking one language and believing in one god has very low symbolic costs; a group speaking twenty languages and believing in five gods finds common symbols extremely hard to find.

**For every new heterogeneous group you add, you owe one more debt of "reweaving the common narrative."** The debt can be repaid, but it takes time.

### Mechanism 2: Institutions have bandwidth

**Governance has bandwidth.** The information a center can effectively process, the demands it can effectively answer, the orders it can effectively carry out — all are finite.

The larger the circle, the longer the chain of information transmission, and the more severe the distortion; the more plural the interests, the slower the consensus; the greater the distance, the higher the cost of oversight.

When **the scale of governance exceeds institutional capacity**, loss of control follows: the center's orders cannot reach the grass roots, the grass roots' problems cannot reach the center. The ruler then has only two paths — either devolve power, but devolution breeds centrifugal force; or concentrate by force, but forced concentration inevitably ossifies, inevitably relies on fear.

**The Soviet Union and late Rome both, in essence, hit the wall of bandwidth.**

### Mechanism 3: The trade-off between diversity and solidarity

Taken together, the first two point to a cold trade-off:

> **You can choose a position between "the most intimate solidarity" and "the broadest accommodation," but you cannot have the maximum of both at once.**

This is not a question of left or right; it is a structural question. Any plan claiming "both maximum inclusivity and maximum cohesion," if it does not say what mechanism achieves both at once, is empty talk.

## 4. Costs and Counterexamples

Two things must be admitted.

**First, expanding too fast equals disintegration.** The Soviet Union tried to rush a new nation into being, and nothing grew. When an order absorbs new members, if **the common narrative and institutional capacity have not yet caught up**, what it swallows is not strength but future fissures. **A circle is not better the bigger it is, but better if it "holds."**

**Second, expanding too slowly equals ossification.** An order that refuses to absorb any new members loses vitality, loses fresh blood, loses the flexibility to respond to change. A closed circle looks stable but is in fact choosing a path of slow death. The maritime bans and closing of the country in the late Ming and Qing are a specimen of this direction.

**So the question is not "expand or not," but "at what speed, with what structure."**

One possible way out is a **layered structure of concentric circles**:

- The core layer: agreeing on the most fundamental set of rules and narrative — this is the hard core of "we";
- The middle layer: sharing interests and rules of exchange, without requiring cultural and religious uniformity;
- The outer ring: connected through trade, treaties, alliances, allowing difference to exist.

**Different distances come with different obligations.** The closer to the core, the weightier the rights and responsibilities; the farther out, the looser but broader the ties. **This way, the circle can be very large without losing focus because it is large.** Chapter 9 will discuss how this structure operates.

## 5. Echoes in the Age of AI

AI arrives in the middle of this tension, as if pressing the accelerator and the brake at once.

**On one hand, it lowers coordination costs.** Machine translation lets people of different languages communicate directly; algorithmic scheduling makes collaboration among hundreds of millions possible; automated execution makes the cost of cross-regional contracts plummet. **Technically, for the first time humanity has the conditions to extend "we" to the civilizational, even global, level.**

**On the other hand, it intensifies fragmentation.** Recommendation algorithms feed you according to your preferences, locking you into a homogeneous information cocoon; people on platforms see more and more only those "like themselves," and less and less the "dissimilar." **Shared experience is deconstructed into countless parallel private realities.**

More troubling: **the same technology can be used to forge a common narrative or to manufacture division.** Those who control the algorithms can both efficiently spread a single "we" and efficiently deepen every fissure. The boundary is not in the machine; it is in its ownership.

> **AI will not automatically expand the circle, nor automatically contract it. It only makes both "expanding" and "contracting" easier, faster, harder to reverse.**

So the question returns to the old place: **who holds it, and whom does it serve.**

## 6. Chapter Summary

Expanding the circle is not a costless undertaking. Its limit is not written on the map of territory but on **the carrying capacity of identity and the bandwidth of institutions.**

> **For every inch the circle grows, you owe an inch of identity; whether you can repay that debt decides whether expansion is growth or disintegration.**

Rome traded division for survival; the Soviet Union repaid its debt of rapid expansion with disintegration; the EU is now paying the cost of backlash for its integration.

**Great harmony is not free solidarity. It is solidarity held open — and holding it open takes time, institutions, and the patience to concede one's share.**
