# Chapter 5 · The Doctrine of Moral Superiority: The Most Efficient Self-Destruct Device

Large-scale atrocities in history were rarely done secretly by bad people.

They were mostly done openly by good people, and while doing them, they believed they stood on the side of justice.

This is not rhetoric; it is a recurring structure: **the perpetrator does not think himself cruel — he thinks he is purifying, saving, carrying out the will of heaven.**

An order's most dangerous enemy is often not its external rival, but the inner certainty that "we are more right than you."

## 1. The Core Claim

> **Self-appointed moral superiority is an order's most dangerous self-destruct device.**
> **It first hands you a license of "justice," then has you use that license to tear down your own foundations with your own hands.**

To see this clearly, one must first distinguish two things:

- **Morality**: a code that binds everyone, including myself.
- **Moral superiority**: an identity that claims I (or we) are born above the code, so the code governs others, not me.

The former builds order; the latter destroys it. This chapter will show how the latter turns a community into a slaughterhouse, and in the end turns back to devour itself.

## 2. The Evidence

**The Crusades.**

In 1095 the pope called on Christians to march to "recover" the Holy Land; the participants' slogan was "the will of God." The movement lasted nearly two hundred years, and the massacres of civilians and sackings of cities were recorded by participants as part of a sacred cause. **Killing heretics was not a sin but a merit.** Violence that can issue pardons has no ceiling.

**The Inquisition.**

The medieval Inquisition targeted "heresy" — not treason, but impurity of belief. Its horror lay not in the cruelty of its punishments, but in **the way it criminalized thought itself.** The interrogator was not punishing an act but putting a soul on trial. When "do you believe correctly" can be grounds to put you to death, all independent thought becomes evidence of guilt. **It served not order, but purity.**

**The Nazi "purification."**

The Nazis' entire vocabulary rested on words like "purification," "hygiene," "healthy nation." Its core logic was not "we will defeat the enemy" but "we will remove the contamination." Jews, Roma, the disabled, homosexuals were systematically struck from the category of "human" and sent to concentration camps. **This was not hatred out of control; it was an ideology that believed it was curing a disease.**

**The Khmer Rouge's "purity."**

In 1975 the Khmer Rouge seized power in Cambodia and carried out a so-called "pure" revolution. Cities were emptied; intellectuals, teachers, doctors, and anyone who spoke a foreign language were cleared out as "stale people." It wanted to create overnight a new society with no classes and no contamination. The result: the movement not only slaughtered vast numbers of its own countrymen but also kept purging its own cadres — **because purity has no endpoint; whoever is pure today may be an impurity tomorrow.**

**The colonizers' "civilizing" mission.**

During modern European expansion, a self-packaging story became popular: the French called it the "civilizing mission," the British "the white man's burden." Plunder was described as enlightenment, conquest as rescue. **The function of this narrative was to translate robbery into benevolence**, leaving the side that inflicted violence with a clear conscience — even a sense of mission.

These five cases differ in era, region, and name. But their structure is identical: **first declare yourself the representative of justice, then declare the other side unworthy of being human, and finally, violence becomes a duty.**

Note especially: most of these participants were not born evil. The Crusades had devout peasants, the Inquisition had conscientious judges, the Nazi ranks had dutiful civil servants, the Khmer Rouge had idealistic youth, the colonial ranks had people who sincerely believed they came to "civilize." **Their problem was not a lack of morality, but that morality's direction had been locked inside the circle.** The higher "our" moral standard is raised, the easier it becomes to dehumanize "them" entirely — two motions, two sides of one coin.

## 3. The Mechanism

### Mechanism 1: First Cancel the Other's Personhood, Then Authorize Atrocity

Ordinary people find it hard to do violence to a "person." To make people commit systematic atrocities, the first step is not to hand them weapons, but to **first cancel the other's personhood.**

So the other is called "vermin," "tumor," "impurity," "barbarian." These words are not rhetoric; they are switches. Once the other is downgraded to an object, a disease, a contamination, the gate in the perpetrator's mind that says "you cannot do this to a person" opens automatically.

> **Every atrocity's preceding step is drawing a circle; before drawing the circle comes striking those outside it off the list of "human."**

### Mechanism 2: Moral Superiority Is Unfalsifiable

This is its deadliest feature.

If a person says "I am seeking profit," you can refute him with facts. But if a person says "I am executing the will of heaven," **you cannot refute him with any fact** — because in his eyes, your opposing him is itself proof that you stand on the side of evil.

Every success of violence is explained as a victory of justice; every setback as a test of justice. **This logic has no exit.** The crueler the participants, the purer they feel. People who speak this language will never wake up one day and realize: I was wrong.

### Mechanism 3: Purification Turns Inward and Devours Itself

Violence directed outward will in the end turn inward.

The reason is simple: "purity" has no endpoint. If the standard is absolute purity, then any disobedience, any hesitation, anyone "not one hundred percent loyal" becomes a new impurity. So the purge extends from enemies to allies, then to comrades-in-arms, and finally reaches the enforcers themselves.

The Khmer Rouge's repeated purging of its own cadres is this mechanism. The later the Inquisition went, the more its trials targeted insiders. **An order whose highest value is purity will in the end devour itself because "no one is pure enough."**

## 4. Costs and Counterexamples

Let it be said plainly: **the problem is not morality, but turning morality into identity.**

Moral fervor itself can mobilize tremendous good.

- Britain's abolitionist movement rested precisely on a group of people treating "slavery is immoral" as a non-negotiable conviction. They spent decades and finally drove through legislation abolishing the slave trade and slavery.
- Rescue in disaster, nursing in plague, aid to strangers — behind these, too, is a strong moral impulse.

**If moral superiority were all bad, these would be inexplicable.**

Where is the difference? Between **identity** and **choice**:

> **The identity statement is: "I am good by nature."**
> **The choice statement is: "I can do good things, but I may go wrong at any time."**

The former issues itself an indulgence, so it is one step from atrocity. The latter leaves itself room for reflection, so it will not spiral out of control.

Peter Singer made a related argument in *The Expanding Circle*: humanity's moral capacity originally covered only the small circle nearby, but through reason and empathy that circle can expand outward generation by generation. **The doctrine of moral superiority is precisely the opposite of expanding the circle — it does not push the circle outward but treats "being right" as a wall, building the circle ever narrower.** A truly durable order needs a wide circle that can accommodate "I might be wrong," not a narrow circle with nothing left but "we are right."

This also bears on the width of the circle. **The "narrow altruism" of a saint at home and a butcher abroad is the extreme of drawing the circle too narrow.** A person can be infinitely gentle to family and utterly merciless to those outside the circle with no inner conflict — because in his morality, those outside the circle are not people at all. Moral superiority is at bottom **mistaking the boundary of the circle for the boundary of the good.**

## 5. Echoes in the Age of AI

Today the religious kind of "purity" is rare, but it survived by changing its skin.

**Technological superiority.** The side that commands algorithms easily slides toward an implicit conclusion — "we hold the data, therefore we are more rational, more correct." So decisions no longer need to persuade those affected; they need only "trust the model."

**Data superiority.** When one side can see everyone's data and the other can see only its own, the former naturally feels it "sees more completely," and therefore "is more qualified to decide for others." **This is information asymmetry disguised as moral superiority.**

**The arrogance of algorithmic justice.** Handing "justice" to an opaque model is to say "whatever the system computes is right." But models are trained on historical data, and **the biases in history are faithfully inherited by the model, then given a coating of algorithmic "objectivity."**

When "I am right" becomes "my model is right," moral superiority upgrades to **technological superiority.** It is more hidden, less questioned, because it wears the cloak of science and carries a string of decimal places.

But the mechanism has not changed: **first declare yourself the representative of correctness, then cancel the other side's right to speak, and finally, harm gets written off as optimization.**

The only way to block this path is to preserve within the technology a place for "I might be wrong": let the model be questioned, let decisions be interrogated, let those affected have a voice. **A system that does not permit itself to be contradicted, however clever, is repeating the old road of the Crusades.**

## 6. Chapter Summary

> **The terror of the doctrine of moral superiority is not that it does evil, but that it does evil while being certain it is doing good.**

The boundary of morality is the boundary of the circle. Narrow the circle until nothing remains but "we are right," and sooner or later it shrinks small enough to squeeze you out too.

**A lasting order does not rely on "we are better than you," only on "we count more people in."**
