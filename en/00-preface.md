# Preface · A Ruler's Question

Someone once asked me this question:

> "If by sacrificing some people I can keep my position and prolong my life, should I do it?"

My answer then was: **No — but not because of morality. Because of arithmetic.**

That trade runs at a loss on your own time scale: what you cut down is a renewable resource, and what you buy is a permanent deficit of legitimacy. Every time you cut, the people who remain lie to you more, your information gets worse, and your knife eventually turns toward you.

**This book is the full version of that answer.**

Let me state first what it is **not**:

- It is **not** a moral tract urging you to be good and to believe that "good is rewarded";
- It is **not** a utopian manual that assumes human nature is good;
- It does **not** claim that "the moral are sure to win" — **on the contrary, it holds that this belief is one of the deadliest illusions in history.** Chapter 5 is devoted to it.

What is it, then? It is a book **about durability**. It asks only one question:

> **What kind of order can outlive the person who made it?**

And its answer is this: a durable order does not depend on how clever the ruler is, how strong the army is, how advanced the technology is. It depends on something plainer — **how many people it counts as "we."**

This variable I call **the radius of the circle**.

---

The core claim of this book can be stated in three sentences:

1. **Every order is built on a circle** — who is inside, who is outside, what morality applies within, what rules apply without;
2. **The radius of the circle determines the lifespan of the order** — a narrow circle is held up by fear and extraction: explosive, and quick to die; a wide circle is held up by credibility and reciprocity: slow to start, and extremely durable;
3. **Expanding the circle is not charity; it is the shrewdest self-interest** — but its payback period is measured in decades and centuries, so the short-sighted never see it, and therefore always lose the long race.

**I am not arguing that one "ought to be merciful." I am reporting a regularity: extractive orders die early systematically; inclusive orders live long systematically.** The victory of benevolence and righteousness is not a moral verdict; it is a statistical result.

This sounds like common sense. Yet historically, the great majority of rulers chose the opposite path **at the critical moment**. Why?

Because **in the short run, the narrow circle is always the better deal.** Purging dissenters works immediately; expanding the circle takes decades to pay back. **A rational ruler who lives in the present will almost inevitably go narrow.** And their descendants will pay for that short-term ledger.

So the real subject of this book is not "goodness." It is **"time"**:

> **The time span you make decisions for is the only size of circle you can see.**

A ruler accountable only to himself can see only his own life.
One accountable to a dynasty can see three generations.
Only one accountable to a civilization can see the circle.

---

## Why Now

This book is written today because of a new variable appearing for the first time in three thousand years: **AI.**

In the past, however brutal a ruler, he had to keep the people — taxes had to be paid, grain grown, wars fought. **The people were means of production, so even out of self-interest the ruler had to feed them.**

AI is shaking that premise. When machines can replace the economic function of the great majority, the ruler has, for the first time, a dangerous fantasy:

> **"I no longer need them."**

This book sets out to show that **this fantasy is the fastest-killing of all ruling fantasies.** Because there is one thing AI can never produce for a ruler — **legitimacy**. And a people stripped of function is precisely the sole source of legitimacy.

Technology can make rule more efficient, or more brittle. **What decides which way it goes is not technology. It is the circle.**

## How to Read This Book

It is structured in four parts:

**Part One, "Foundations,"** shows how the circle, credibility, reciprocity, and institutions form the substrate of every order.

**Part Two, "Traps,"** dissects three self-destructive modes: the doctrine of moral superiority, short-term predation, and the purge. These three chapters are the coldest, and the most useful.

**Part Three, "Expansion,"** takes up the real costs of expanding the circle: the tension between universal unity and solidarity, how to accommodate the unlike-minded, and why the gains must be divided.

**Part Four, "The Present,"** places all of this in the age of AI and lands on the most concrete question of all: **what you yourself can do.**

Every chapter has a fixed skeleton: the core claim, the historical evidence, the mechanism, the costs and counterexamples, and the echoes in the age of AI. **I insist on writing the "costs" and "counterexamples," because any one-sided argument is a fraud** — including my own.

---

## One Line I Will Not Cross

This book holds to a single line:

> **Write no uncertain history. Better to write less than to fabricate.**

I cite history not for ornament, but because **a mechanism can be tested only in a concrete event.** A judgment that "the wide circle lasts longer" is just a pretty phrase unless it can be verified again and again in the fall of Rome, the collapse of the Soviet Union, the dynastic cycle.

Pretty phrases: this book wants none.

**Now we begin with the most basic word of all: the circle.**
