# Chapter 12 · From Rule by Man to Self-Governance: The Last Mile of Order

An edict leaves the capital. What does it become by the time it reaches a border village?

It is translated, intercepted, distorted, hollowed out. The one who issues it believes he rules that land; in truth he rules only a map. What actually decides how the people on that land divide water, borrow oxen, deal with a chicken thief, is something else — **and that something was never written by him.**

This is the common predicament of every large order: **the top can draw the outline, but cannot govern the last mile.** And whether an order lives or dies is decided precisely at the last mile.

## 1. The Core Claim

> **A durable order is not designed. It is "grown" out of countless rules at the grassroots.**

The higher you look, the more abstract order becomes — law, constitution, ideology. The lower you go, the more concrete — who digs this well first, how many sheep this pasture can hold, who repairs this road. Abstract order is backed by violence; concrete order is sustained by **the people who live there, themselves.**

A regime can put its orders on paper, but it cannot put enforcement at every village gate. **It can do only two things: leave it to the grassroots, or use countless officials to force it.** The first is called self-governance; the second is rule by man, extended.

This chapter will show: **self-governance is not an ideal, it is arithmetic. It is the only last mile a durable order can afford.**

## 2. The Evidence

**Ostrom and the discovery of self-governance.** Mainstream twentieth-century economics believed a pessimistic conclusion: common resources will inevitably be overused and finally exhausted — the tragedy of the commons. The logic is simple: the pasture belongs to everyone, the benefit of one more sheep goes to you alone, the cost of its degradation is shared by all, so everyone adds one more. The conclusion: either privatize, or let the state manage it.

Elinor Ostrom did not believe the either-or. She and her team went out into the real world to look: irrigation systems in Nepal, lobster fisheries in Maine and Nova Scotia, mountain commons in Switzerland and Japan, ancient water canals in Spain. She found that **many communities did not wait for the state at all; they grew their own rules** — who may use how much water in which season, who may enter which fishery, how violations are punished, who arbitrates disputes.

These rules were not brought in from outside; local people hammered them out over centuries. She distilled them into a set of design principles: **clear boundaries, rules suited to local conditions, participation by those bound by the rules, monitoring, graduated sanctions, low-cost conflict resolution, recognition of local rule-making by higher authorities, and nested layers of governance.** For this work she won the Nobel Prize in economics — **the first time the academy formally recognized that self-governance is not backwardness, but an effective technology of governance.**

**Fisheries and irrigation: neighbor watches neighbor.** In those lobster fisheries, if you fish across the line, every nearby fisherman can see it; the punishment is not a fine but cutting off your credibility at that harbor. In those irrigation systems, farmers take water by turns and clear silt together; if you steal water, the neighbors know by next day. **Monitoring here costs almost nothing, because the monitor and the monitored live together, in constant sight of each other.** By contrast, send an official from the capital to manage water, and he neither knows which canal is silted nor can tell who is lying.

**Imperial power stops at the county; village covenants and village communities.** In China's imperial era, the official establishment usually reached only to the county level. Below that, the vast countryside was long sustained by clans, gentry, the lijia, and village communities. The Lü Clan Village Covenant of the Song, and the covenants promoted by Wang Yangming in the Ming, were efforts to localize moral rules and have neighbors exhort and restrain one another. The differential mode of association that Fei Xiaotong described in From the Soil is exactly this localized order, spreading outward ring by ring from the self. **The state nominally owned everything, yet actual governance was long outsourced to the grassroots.** The medieval European village community, the shared pastures of the Alps, the irrigation tribunals of Spain are another version of the same thing.

**Cooperatives: writing the rules into ownership.** The cooperatives of Mondragón in Spain and of Italy's Emilia-Romagna region show that self-governance can extend into production. The workers are both employees and owners; major decisions are made by the members together; surplus is distributed by rule. That such organizations survive for decades or even a century depends not on moral nobility but on **binding three things together: who benefits, who decides, who monitors.**

These cases span Eurasia and a thousand years and point to one conclusion: **wherever the last mile is left to the local people themselves, order is cheaper and more durable.**

## 3. The Mechanism

### Mechanism 1: Local information is held only by locals

Governance needs information. The crucial part of it is **local, fragile, unreportable.**

Which stretch of which canal clogs easily, which pasture should carry fewer sheep this year, which family is truly in difficulty and which is feigning poverty — this knowledge exists only in the daily experience of those involved. Hayek long ago pointed out that the most important knowledge in society is **local knowledge dispersed among countless individuals, which cannot be centrally collected.** A central planner can obtain statistics, but not these details.

**So top-down rule is inherently information-poor.** It is not governing; it is forcing a crude model onto a complex reality. The advantage of self-governance is not moral — it is **the location of information**: the decision-maker lives inside the information.

### Mechanism 2: The cost of monitoring rises with distance

A misdeed farther from the monitor is harder to detect, and once detected, harder to prove.

A monitor at the top must cross thousands of kilometers and pass through layer after layer of agents before touching the truth. And each layer of agents may lie, hollow out, take a cut. **The cost of monitoring rises exponentially with distance.** The cost of sending one person is far higher than letting a hundred neighbors watch one another — because a neighbor's monitoring is nearly free.

This is the flip side of what Olson argued in The Logic of Collective Action: in a large group everyone free-rides, because "whether I pitch in or not, the result is the same." But **a small group, face-to-face, interacting repeatedly, can overcome free-riding** — because betrayal is seen, and what is seen is remembered. Axelrod's work on repeated games shows the same thing: **so long as the game will be played again, cooperation can hold.** A self-governing group is exactly that kind of repeated game — "the days are long, and you cannot run away."

### Mechanism 3: Self-governance produces "a sense of ownership"

This one runs deepest.

Obeying rules set by others and being bound by rules you helped make are two entirely different kinds of obedience.

The first kind is paid for by the ruler — he must monitor, punish, constantly prove he is not to be crossed. **It is an expense.** The second is borne by the person himself — because the rule holds a share of him, and obeying it is keeping a promise to himself. **It is an asset.**

> **People will not die for rules others wrote; only for rules they signed themselves.**

This is the first chapter's line made concrete: **fear is a consumable; consent is a renewable resource.** And self-governance is the lowest-cost machine for producing consent. It turns each person from "an object to be governed" into "a master who governs" — **and a sense of ownership is the bottom layer of consent, the layer hardest for anything external to replace.**

## 4. Costs and Counterexamples

Omit this section and the chapter becomes utopian propaganda. Self-governance fails, and fails often.

**First, free-riding.** Some labor on the canal, others enjoy the result. When enough people share and too few contribute, self-governance collapses from within. Olson's logic still holds here: not every group can solve this on its own.

**Second, elite capture.** The more common failure is not that no one manages things, but that **those who manage rewrite the rules in their own favor.** The village strongman, the clan's big households, the party with resources, gradually seize the power to make and interpret the rules. The common resource seems to remain, but has in fact become the private property of a few. Ostrom herself warned repeatedly: **self-governance is not automatically fair; it requires monitoring and checks and balances.** Her "graduated sanctions" and "conflict-resolution mechanisms" exist precisely to prevent this.

**Third, a small circle turns exclusionary, and does evil.** A self-governing body with clear boundaries is often harsh toward those outside "us." Clan feuds, local protectionism, oppressive village rules, village bosses ruling the village — these are the tumors self-governance grows. **Self-governance can produce consent, and it can produce closure and brutality.**

**Fourth, isolated self-governance does not last.** This is the most critical limit. If a self-governing unit has no higher order to fall back on, one of two ends awaits: it is swallowed by greater violence, or it arms itself and becomes a predator. **Hence Ostrom's final principle: "nesting."** The self-governing unit must nest, layer by layer, within a larger structure of governance. The local handles the everyday; the higher handles what the local cannot: repelling outside enemies, arbitrating conflicts between localities, halting tyranny within a locality.

> **Self-governance does not mean the state withdraws; it means order is layered.** The top provides legitimacy, boundaries, and final arbitration; the grassroots provides information, execution, and a sense of ownership. Lose either end and the order comes apart.

## 5. Echoes in the Age of AI

Modern technology gives Ostrom's rules a chance to scale for the first time — because it drives down both the **organizing cost** and the **monitoring cost** of self-governance.

Open-source communities let complete strangers maintain a body of code together, relying on public commit records, clear contribution boundaries, visible graduated permissions. Wikipedia is built by countless strangers cooperating, relying on traceable edit histories, public dispute-resolution processes, rules anyone can audit. These map almost item by item onto the principles of self-governance: **clear boundaries, transparent monitoring, an outlet for conflict, accountable power.** Blockchain and smart contracts even automate the "penalties within the rules," no longer depending on human goodwill.

**This is one path that empowers the grassroots:** in the past self-governance meant gathering people, holding meetings, keeping accounts, endless wrangling — costs so high many villages could not sustain it; now the tools drive it to nearly zero.

But the same technology can be used in reverse, and that direction is straighter and faster.

**See-through surveillance.** Cameras, sensors, data platforms give the top, for the first time, the ability to **bypass every intermediate layer and look directly at each grassroots unit.** Once the emperor could not reach the village gate; now data flows all the way down, turning every village, every household, every person's behavior into a computable data point.

This looks like a leap in governing capacity. But it has a fatal side effect: **it turns the grassroots "master" back into an "object."** When the top can see everything directly, the grassroots no longer needs to grow its own rules — it need only execute the rules from above. The three mechanisms on which self-governance depends are hollowed out at once:

- Local information is still useful, but no longer **used** by locals;
- monitoring no longer requires neighbors, but is done by algorithms, monopolized by the top;
- and the sense of ownership vanishes — **when a person no longer takes part in making the rules, only obedience remains.**

So the same technology points to two fates: **it can pave the last mile as a top-down conduit, or as a network that grows from the bottom up.** The difference is not in the technology itself but in **who owns the data, who has the right to hold power accountable, and whether the grassroots is entitled to say "no."**

This is Chapter 11's judgment landing at the grassroots: **AI can expand the circle and can narrow it; likewise it can empower the village community and can see straight through it.** The deciding power lies not with the algorithm but with institutions.

## 6. Chapter Summary

> **The foundation of a durable order is self-governance; its ceiling is the circle. The top decides how large it can grow; the grassroots decides whether it can live.**

How long a regime lasts does not depend on whether it can deliver orders to every village gate, but on **whether it dares let that village gate decide some things for itself.**

Qin Shi Huang gathered power to the extreme; it died in the second generation.
The Han handed "we" to people who read the same books and kept the same village rules; it lived four hundred years.

**The last mile is always walked, to the end, by the people who live there.**
