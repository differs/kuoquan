# Chapter 10 · Dividing the Gains: Who Bears the Cost of Expansion

For an order to expand, someone must contribute labor, money, and lives.

The question was never "should anyone sacrifice" — but **after the sacrifice, whose are the fruits**.

> "Follow me, and one day you'll have your share."

If this is honored, it is a promise, a binding agent.
If it is not honored, it is a deception, a fuse.

This chapter is about that word, "share."

## 1. The Core Claim

> **Expanding the circle requires dividing the gains.**
> **Expansion without dividing the gains is deception; and deception inevitably provokes backlash.**

An order that drags outsiders in but gives them no share — how is it different from a band of robbers? **Dragging people in completes only the form of the circle; dividing the gains is the substance of the circle.**

> **Identity is not established by talking, but by dividing.**

## 2. The Evidence

### Late Rome: the wealth pump drained the republic

Beneath the military and political surface of Rome's decline ran an economic undercurrent: **extreme concentration of wealth.**

Expansion brought vast lands, slaves, and spoils, and almost all of it flowed to the upper strata. Small and middling freeholders went bankrupt, their land swallowed by great estates, a large population reduced to dependents or the urban proletariat. **What supported the republic was precisely those freeholders with land and the obligation of military service. When they vanished, the foundation of the republic emptied out.**

The Gracchi tried to reform — redistributing public land, limiting land concentration, protecting the small farmer. The result? Both died by political violence. **The vested interests would rather destroy the order than concede a single share.**

Thereafter Rome marched step by step toward civil war, dictatorship, empire. **By the late empire, the tax burden pressed on the few middle and lower strata that could still bear it, while the immensely rich and great landowners found ways to evade, and the gap between rich and poor tore ever wider.** Some researchers regard this as a key link in Western Rome's structural fragility — **not that the army was insufficient, but that the interior had already fallen apart.**

> **An order unwilling to divide the gains will first drain its backbone, then find it has no one left to use.**

### Latin America: the inequality colonialism left behind

Latin America's long-term inequality is widely traced to the colonial era.

What Spain and Portugal built in the Americas were **extractive** institutions: land and labor highly concentrated in the hands of a few colonial elites, the indigenous forced into the labor system, communal lands eroded. **The institutions were designed from the start to "let the many get less."**

After independence, political independence did not bring an overturning of the economic structure. Land, education, and political power long remained in the hands of a few. **Inequalitarian institutions reproduce themselves through path dependence** — the rich have the resources to shape the rules, and the rules make the rich richer.

Some studies rank Latin America among the world's most unequal regions in income distribution. **Its turmoil and stagnation are not accidental; they are the result of institutions long tilted to one side.**

### The Nordics: stability bought by conceding shares

The opposite specimen is the Nordic countries.

From the twentieth century, Sweden, Denmark, Norway, and others gradually built systems of high taxes, high welfare, and universal redistribution: cheap or free education and healthcare, broad unemployment insurance, pensions, childcare support. **The market was not abolished, enterprises were not eliminated, but the fruits of growth were systematically divided among the many.**

The result was not stagnation, but **long-term stability and relatively high social trust**. Workers were willing to accept technological change and structural adjustment because the costs of those changes were shared; employers were willing to accept high taxes because what they got in return was a low-conflict, high-skill, trustworthy workforce.

**"Conceding shares" here is not a moral choice; it is a deal both sides can calculate clearly.**

### The Chinese dynastic cycle: land concentration and peasant rebellion

Across China's two-thousand-year dynastic cycle runs one recurring thread: **land concentration.**

A new dynasty typically rebuilt the smallholder after war, with land relatively dispersed and the tax base solid. But after long peace, land gradually concentrated into the hands of powerful families, officials, and clans, and smallholders fell into tenancy or became refugees. **The state's tax base drained away with it, while the landless grew more numerous.**

When batch after batch of people who could no longer survive gathered together, the dynasty entered its countdown. From the Yellow Turbans and Huang Chao to Li Zicheng, large-scale peasant rebellions were almost always the product of sharpened land contradictions.

**"Equal-field" policies were not charity; they were the state preserving its own tax base.** And each collapse of a dynasty was, in essence, a violent reset after the mechanism for dividing the gains had utterly failed.

### Turchin: the wealth pump

The historian Peter Turchin offered a powerful summary: the **"wealth pump."**

Its core is **structural-demographic theory**: when the distribution of social resources is long tilted toward elites, a "pump" continuously draws wealth from ordinary people into the hands of a few elites. There are two consequences:

First, **ordinary people are drained**, living conditions worsen, resentment accumulates;
second, **elite overproduction** — because elite positions are limited while the upper stratum keeps swelling, elites fight fiercely among themselves for scarce opportunities.

**When discontent below and infighting above appear at the same time, society enters the critical zone of upheaval.** Turchin uses this to explain numerous political crises from Rome to the modern era. **The sharp edge of this theory is that it sees collapse as the inevitable result of a distribution problem, not the accidental blunder of some villain.**

## 3. The Mechanism

### Mechanism 1: Redistribution is the source of legitimacy

**Why does a person obey?** One fundamental reason: **he feels he has a stake in this order.**

With a stake, he has a motive to defend it; without one, he has only a motive to rebel. **"Dividing the gains" is not a cost of order, but an investment in order** — what it buys is legitimacy, and legitimacy is what fear can never buy.

Chapter 1 said: "Fear is a consumable; consent is a renewable resource." **Dividing the gains is the machine that produces consent.**

### Mechanism 2: Excessive elite extraction drains the middle and lower strata

Elite extraction is not itself the problem — every society has elites. **The problem is the speed and ceiling of extraction.**

When extraction exceeds the reproductive capacity of the middle and lower strata, it becomes **killing the goose that lays the golden eggs**: the tax base withers, consumption collapses, labor flees, and in the end even the elites lose anything left to extract.

**Chapter 6 discussed stationary bandits and roving bandits: sustainable exploitation is better than destructive plunder.** The mechanism of dividing the gains is, in essence, constraining "plunder" into "sustainable exploitation," so that the whole system does not destroy itself.

### Mechanism 3: Refusing to concede will ultimately lose everything

This is the coldest arithmetic in the book:

> **When those excluded become numerous enough, their combined force will exceed the cost of maintaining the exclusion.**

At that critical point, the ruler has only two choices: **either concede, or be overthrown.** And most rulers who refuse to concede early end up with neither option — they are thoroughly liquidated in violence, losing even the part they might have kept.

**Charles I, Louis XVI, Tsar Nicholas II walked the same road.** They did not fall to reform; they fell to **refusing timely reform**.

## 4. Costs and Counterexamples

The counterexamples must be written down honestly, or this chapter becomes a sermon on egalitarianism.

**First, redistribution has an incentive cost.** Divide too sharply, tax too high, make effort irrelevant, and people lose the motive to create. **Excessive equality kills the cake itself.**

**Second, "equality" is not better the higher it is, but should be "sufficient and sustainable."** The Nordic model endures because alongside high redistribution it preserves market vitality, competitive mechanisms, and entrepreneurial freedom. **It concedes shares, but does not wipe out the elites.**

**Third, excessive division of gains also creates dependency and ossification.** Excessive welfare can rob some people of the motive to enter the labor market and crowd out resources that should go to investment and innovation.

So the real question is not "divide or not," but **how much, how, and who decides**.

> **A healthy order is a machine that can both keep creating and keep sharing. Without creation, it starves; without sharing, it burns.**

**Find the balance, do not abolish either side.**

## 5. Echoes in the Age of AI

AI pushes the problem of "dividing the gains" into its sharpest position in history.

The reason is simple: **when wealth is increasingly produced by data and compute rather than by people, then "who owns the data and compute" directly determines "who gets a share of the money."**

In the past, labor was the ticket by which most people participated in distribution. **In the age of AI, that ticket may be monopolized by the few institutions that own models, compute, and data.** If this happens, we will see the largest wealth pump in history — **not drawing toward landlords and nobles, but toward the owners of platforms and compute.**

So the question surfaces: **whose are the dividends?**

- **Universal basic income** — distributing the output created by AI to everyone as unconditional cash. Supporters say it is the floor that sustains consumption and stability; critics say it is costly and may breed idleness.
- **Data dividends** — since everyone's behavioral data is raw material, should the value produced be returned in shares to the producers of the data?
- **A compute tax** — taxing the returns on compute and models for public redistribution, converting "technological dividends" into "social dividends."

**None of these proposals is settled yet; each has its own holes. But they point to the same ancient question, merely in a new dimension: how are the fruits of expansion to be divided?**

> **The greatest political question of the AI age is not whether machines will rebel, but whether the wealth machines create will belong only to their owners.**

And the answer history gives is as ever: **without dividing the gains, there is no legitimacy; without legitimacy, no technology, however efficient, can hold an order together.**

## 6. Chapter Summary

Expanding the circle is not just pulling people in. **Pulling people in without giving them a share is a fraud, and the most easily detonated kind of fraud.**

> **How long an order survives depends on whether it is willing to divide the fruits among those who hold it up.**

Rome died of refusing to concede; Latin America was locked in by the path of extractive institutions; the Nordics bought long stability by conceding shares; the Chinese dynasties collapsed again and again in the cycle of land concentration. Turchin's "wealth pump" tells us: **collapse is almost never an accident; it is the maturity date of a distribution imbalance.**

**The cost of expansion must ultimately be divided by someone. The only difference is: do you divide it actively, or are you passively divided away to nothing.**
