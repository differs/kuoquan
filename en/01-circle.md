# Chapter 1 · The Circle: The First Principle of Order

Imagine two tribes separated by a river.

The hunters on this bank are generous enough to give away their last piece of meat to their fellows; when a stranger on the other bank falls into the water, they stand on the shore and watch, unmoved.

**They are not being hypocritical.** In their world, the people on the other bank do not count as "people" at all.

Human morality has never been one set of rules but **two**: one for inside, one for outside. Understanding this is the starting point for understanding every order — and every atrocity.

## 1. The Core Claim

> **People's behavior is not determined by "whether they are moral," but by "who counts as one of us."**
> **Every atrocity is preceded by the drawing of a circle.**

Order is not a choice between "good" and "evil"; it is **the drawing of a line** — benevolence and righteousness inside the line, rules outside it. **Where the line is drawn determines how long the order lives.**

## 2. The Evidence: Saints Inside the Circle, Butchers Outside

Consider three real worlds:

**Inside Nazi Germany.** An ordinary German could be a loving father, a reliable neighbor, a loyal friend to his colleagues — and at the same time unmoved by everything happening in the camps. Scholars who later studied this phenomenon found it was not "hypocrisy": in their moral system, Jews, Roma, and Slavs **were not within the category of "people" at all.** Only after the other's personhood was revoked could atrocity find ordinary, everyday executors.

**The citizens and slaves of Athens.** Athens is honored as the cradle of democracy: citizens debated as equals in the agora and prized dignity and freedom. What sustained that life was a vast number of slaves — **they were not inside the citizens' moral circle.** The Athenians' cruelty to slaves and their warmth toward their own came from the same heart.

**The Mongol Empire's roster.** The Mongols were known for extreme internal loyalty, good faith, and discipline, which let them build the largest empire of their time. But outward, the choices they offered conquered cities were often extremely brutal. **The same organizational capacity is efficiency inside, and destruction outside.**

These cases together show one thing: **morality does not disappear; it is confined by the circle.** People's kindness inside the circle is real, and their coldness outside it is real too — and between the two there is **no psychological conflict whatsoever**.

## 3. The Mechanism: How the Circle Works

### Mechanism 1: The Tribe Is the Brain's Factory Setting

The morality humans evolved is a combination of "altruism inward, hostility outward," which scholars call **parochial altruism**. Taking a blade for a child of one's own group is instinct; staying wary of a stranger from another tribe, ready to strike at any moment, is instinct too. **Both reactions run on the same neural circuitry.**

And the upper limit on how many people one can truly treat as "people" is about one hundred and fifty — a number that comes from the anthropologist Dunbar's research on the social brain. Beyond that number, the other is no longer a "person" but a statistic.

**So every large order is an engineering project against instinct.** It must use some external thing to force the circle of "who counts as us" open, far beyond a hundred and fifty people.

### Mechanism 2: Holding the Circle Open Requires "External Things"

What are the tools for holding the circle open? **Gods, ancestors, language, law, money, flags.**

These apparently unrelated things share one feature: **they make a person willing to obey the rules for the sake of someone he cannot see.**

- A shared god makes you and a stranger a thousand miles away belong to the same "we";
- A shared law makes you trust that a stranger will keep his word;
- A shared currency makes you trust that the note in your hand will still be worth something tomorrow.

**Order is not held up by love; it is pinned down by these "external things."** The stronger the tools, the bigger the circle can be held.

### Mechanism 3: The Narrow Circle Runs on Fear, the Wide Circle on Consent

A circle can be very narrow or very wide, and the cost of ruling is completely different in each case:

- The **narrow circle** runs on fear: you must constantly surveil, suppress, purge. **This is a never-ending expenditure.**
- The **wide circle** runs on consent: people obey because "this order is my own." **This is an asset, not an expenditure.**

> **"Fear is a consumable; consent is a renewable resource."**

This is why **the narrow circle is doomed to be expensive, and an expensive rule is doomed to be short-lived.**

## 4. Costs and Counterexamples

Three things must be made clear, or this chapter becomes a moral sermon.

**First, a wide circle is not weakness.** A wide circle needs violence, law, and borders just the same. What it expands is "who counts as us," **not "there are no enemies."** The idealistic orders that died throughout history are beyond counting — they did not fall to mercy, they fell to naivety.

**Second, the circle cannot expand too fast.** Once expansion exceeds what the institutions can carry, it loses both internal consent and external order at once. The Soviet Union tried to manufacture a "new Soviet man" within a single generation; the result was the collapse of cohesion and community together. **A separate chapter (Chapter 8) treats this.**

**Third, expansion has real costs.** The bigger the circle, the more heterogeneous its interior, the harder to unite, the slower to decide. **Universal unity and solidarity are a real tension.** You cannot have both the tightest cohesion and the broadest inclusion. Acknowledging this tension is where this book differs from cheap idealism.

## 5. Echoes in the Age of AI

AI has fitted the task of "holding the circle open" with two possibilities at once.

**It may hold the circle open to an unprecedented size.** Machine translation, real-time collaboration, algorithmic coordination have for the first time driven the cost of cooperation among billions to a minimum. **Technically, humanity is for the first time capable of extending "we" to everyone.**

**It may also draw the circle shut to an unprecedented narrowness.** The same technology can be used for precise surveillance, predicting dissent, and divide and rule. When a ruler can maintain order by algorithm, he no longer needs consent at all — **and so the circle is pulled back to a tiny handful.**

**The same AI points to two entirely opposite outcomes.** Which path is taken does not depend on the technology; it depends on **who owns it and whom it serves.** This is precisely the question Part Four unfolds.

## 6. Chapter Summary

> **The boundary of order is the boundary of the circle. Where the circle is drawn is how far the order lives.**

How long a regime lives is not a matter of how high its walls are, but of **how many people it makes feel that "this is my own affair."**

Qin Shi Huang built the longest wall, and the dynasty died in its second generation.
The Han expanded "we" to everyone who read the same books, and it lived four hundred years.

**This is not a moral fable. It is arithmetic.**
