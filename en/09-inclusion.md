# Chapter 9 · Accommodating the Dissimilar: The Mechanism of Expansion

Imagine a ruler facing a group of outsiders. He has three choices:

First, kill them or drive them away; second, force them to become his own; third, **let them remain as they are, but bring them into the same order.**

The first two are common. The third is hardest, and most durable.

This chapter is about the third — **accommodation**.

## 1. The Core Claim

> **Accommodation is not assimilation.**
> **True expansion is not turning others into you, but making their differences part of the order.**

Assimilation demands the other abandon himself; accommodation demands only that the other accept common rules.

**The former manufactures silent resentment; the latter manufactures conditional loyalty.** In the long run, the latter wins.

## 2. The Evidence

### Rome: citizenship was given out rung by rung

Rome's most remarkable invention was perhaps not the legions, not the law, but **the ladder of citizenship**.

Early Roman citizenship was precious and exclusionary. But Rome did not lock it shut; it designed intermediate steps: **Latin rights** — a secondary status without the vote but with the conveniences of trade and intermarriage. Allied city-states could obtain it and see the possibility of climbing higher.

This design solved one fatal problem. In the first century BCE, Rome's Italian allies bled and fought for Rome yet could not obtain citizenship, and finally the **Social War** broke out. Rome's response was not to suppress to the end, but to compromise — through law, granting citizenship en masse to its Italian allies.

**Fight a war, then grant rights, and turn the enemy into one of your own.** That bill was far cheaper than killing them all.

By 212 CE, the emperor Caracalla simply granted citizenship to nearly all free men within the empire. **This step turned "Roman" from an ethnic identity into a legal identity.**

> **The empire's secret was not making people into Romans, but making "being a Roman" cheap.**

### China: the imperial examination and "all under heaven"

China took another road, but the logic is the same.

**The imperial examination** was the core device of this logic. It asked nothing about your birth, region, or ethnicity — only whether you could read the same set of books and pass the same set of exams. **It provided an obtainable channel of advancement across class.**

A poor youth from Lingnan, if he mastered that set of classics, had a chance to enter the court. **That channel was his proof of "being included."**

Matched to this was the **view of "all under heaven" (tianxia)**. Tianxia was not a closed ethnic group but an order bounded by civilization and able to extend outward without end. The Hua–Yi distinction was chiefly not about blood, but about **whether one accepted this civilization**. Accept it, and you could come in. This conception let China accommodate vastly different dialects, customs, and peoples across an extremely broad territory while sharing the same script and institutions.

**A single script for the whole realm was the largest-scale project of "common narrative" ever undertaken.**

### America: "melting pot" or "salad bowl"

The largest modern immigrant nation has long argued over one question: **should immigrants be melted down, or preserved?**

The phrase "melting pot" came from the title of a play by the writer Zangwill. It held that immigrants from around the world would ultimately fuse into a brand-new, single American. This is the assimilationist view.

Decades later, the thinker Kallen proposed another view: **cultural pluralism**. He used a metaphor later popularized as the "**salad bowl**" — various cultures like lettuce, tomato, cucumber, mixed in one bowl, each keeping its shape and flavor, yet together making one dish.

**These two lines are still pulling against each other today.** The substance of the argument is the core claim of this chapter: **demand assimilation, or allow coexistence?**

The vague answer history gives is: **the most effective integration is often "unity in the public sphere + pluralism in the private sphere."** The public sphere speaks one language and accepts one constitution; the private sphere keeps its own religion, food, and festivals.

### The Ottomans: writing difference into institutions

The Ottoman **millet system** is the extreme specimen of "institutionalizing difference."

It did not require everyone to assimilate into one nation, but recognized the legal status of religious communities. Each millet had its own leader, handled internal affairs like marriage and burial, and paid taxes and pledged loyalty to the sultan. **The empire handled taxes and loyalty; the community handled life and faith.**

This let the Ottomans rule an extremely plural territory for centuries. Its limit, as Chapter 8 said: **differences institutionalized also meant boundaries hardened.**

### Intermarriage, migration, and trade: soft accommodation

Beyond institutions, there is a slower, deeper force.

**Intermarriage** — children of two groups grow up in two worlds, and are themselves living bridges. **Migration** — when people move, a strange group becomes a neighbor, and abstract hostility becomes concrete human feeling. **Trade** — when your livelihood depends on doing business with the other, you have a real motive to preserve the peace.

**These three things require no one's goodwill, only interest and contact.** They are the cheapest fusion machines.

## 3. The Mechanism

### Mechanism 1: Concentric circles / pluralistic unity

The best structure for accommodation is **concentric circles**, not a great melting pot.

**The core is a small number of rules everyone must accept** — perhaps a constitution, perhaps a common language, perhaps basic loyalty. Outward are the layers where difference may be preserved: your religion, your customs, your community.

> **Pluralistic unity is neither "a heap of loose sand" nor "uniformity in a thousand molds," but "under one body, plural coexistence."**

Chapter 8 said concentric circles are the way out for expansion; here we add their content: **the core must be small and hard, the outer rings large and soft.**

### Mechanism 2: An obtainable identity and channel of advancement

**The key to identity is not who you currently are, but whether you can become "one of us."**

A circle forever shut will make those outside give up and turn to hostility. A circle with an open door and clear steps will make those outside strive to climb — **their striving is itself investing in this order.**

Rome's ladder of citizenship, China's imperial examination, the naturalization process of modern states — all are different versions of this mechanism.

> **Giving a person a path upward keeps him better than giving him a flag.**

### Mechanism 3: Common narrative and ritual

People need a story to place themselves within a larger whole.

**A common narrative** tells you: where we came from, who we are, where we are going. **Ritual** — whether raising a flag, a celebration, or a festival — turns the abstract "we" into a shared, bodily experience.

**Story gives identity its content; ritual gives identity its warmth.** Without either, the circle looks shriveled.

### Mechanism 4: Binding interests

Last, and hardest: **you have a stake here.**

When a person's property, career, security, and future are all bound to this order, he has a real motive to defend it — **not because he loves it, but because he cannot afford to lose it.**

**The highest form of identity is a community of interests.** This is also the theme of Chapter 10: dividing the gains.

## 4. Costs and Counterexamples

Accommodation is not an all-purpose cure without a bottom line.

**First, accommodating too much, too fast, dilutes core identity.** If the speed of entry exceeds the speed at which the common narrative and institutions can digest it, the result is not fusion but **no common "we" at all**. The splits of some multi-ethnic states in rapid transition, the tensions in some European societies that admitted immigrants too quickly — both have tasted this bitterness.

**Second, accommodation can be a technique of rule, not goodwill.** The Ottoman millet system, managing communities separately, objectively also had the effect of **divide and rule** — letting the communities check one another, making the center easier to control. **Between "recognizing difference" and "exploiting difference" there is only a hair's breadth.**

**Third, the condition of the accommodated is not necessarily good.** Preserving difference sometimes means being assigned to an inferior, fixed position. **Pluralism can be respect, or it can be segregation.** America's long history of racial segregation reminds us: **"separate but equal" was never equal.**

> **The quality of accommodation is not measured by whether it allows difference to exist, but by whether it allows difference to rise.**

Difference may be preserved, but the path upward must be open to all. Otherwise "pluralism" merely gives inequality a prettier name.

## 5. Echoes in the Age of AI

AI has fitted the matter of "accommodating the dissimilar" with an unprecedentedly powerful **translation machine**.

**Machine translation is flattening the walls of language.** A Chinese person, an Arab, a Brazilian can, for the first time, directly understand what the others are thinking with almost no common language. **Language, the oldest and most stubborn boundary of the circle, is for the first time penetrable by technology.**

Collaboration tools, real-time subtitles, automatic adaptation of cross-cultural content — all are lowering the cost of interaction between the dissimilar. **In theory, for the first time humanity has the conditions to make "understanding a stranger" cheap.**

But the reverse force is equally powerful.

**Recommendation algorithms push homogeneity to the extreme.** They discover what you like to watch and show you only that, sealing each group into its own echo chamber. **AI can connect you to the dissimilar, or precisely ensure you never encounter it.**

More dangerous still: **the same technology can be used to promote understanding or to manufacture and amplify hatred.** Generated content can cheaply fabricate the other side's atrocities and incite antagonism; precision targeting can feed anger to the people most easily ignited. **The cost of tearing apart has also fallen to an all-time low.**

> **AI makes both "understanding the dissimilar" and "hating the dissimilar" easier. It amplifies existing directions; it does not choose a direction for you.**

The choice still belongs to those who hold this tool — to institutions, to ownership.

## 6. Chapter Summary

The real mechanism of expansion is not grinding everyone into one shape. Quite the opposite:

> **A durable circle has a narrow, hard core and broad, soft edges — it demands loyalty to common rules while letting you keep yourself.**

Rome turned enemies into its own with a ladder of citizenship; China stitched a vast land and multitude into one tianxia with the imperial examination and a common script; the Ottomans wrote difference into institutions with the millet system; and each time, the order that could turn difference into loyalty outlived the order that demanded conformity.

**Accommodation is not weakness; it is the highest technique of expansion.**
